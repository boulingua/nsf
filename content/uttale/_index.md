Norwegian spelling costs a German- or English-speaking learner almost nothing:
the Latin alphabet plus three letters at the end, **æ ø å**. Norwegian
*pronunciation* costs rather more, and most of the cost is paid in the first
fortnight. This short stage front-loads it, so that the A1 units can spend
their time on language rather than on repairing habits formed in week one.
{{< /lead >}}
{{< /hero >}}

## Why a separate stage

Courses in languages with an unfamiliar script open with a script stage.
Norwegian does not need one — a reader of German or English can decode a
Norwegian page on day one and be wrong about almost every word. That is the
problem. The gap between Norwegian spelling and Norwegian speech is wide and
systematic: *hvem* opens with a *v*, *det* ends in a vowel, *skjære* and
*kjære* start in two different places, and vowel length decides whether
*takk* and *tak* are the same word.

None of this is difficult. All of it is invisible if nobody points at it. So
the course points at it once, in a stage of its own, before Unit 1, and then
refers back to these pages whenever a unit's audio raises a question.

## What this stage does not ask of you

**It does not ask you to produce the tonemes.** Norwegian has two pitch
accents. They are not written, and they distinguish real pairs — *bønder*
(farmers) from *bønner* (beans), *tanken* (the thought) from *tanken* (the
tank). This stage describes them, gives you the pairs to listen to, and stops
there. Recognising that Norwegian has a melody is worth having from the start;
being marked on producing it at A1 is not, and no task in A1, A2 or B1 grades
it.

**It does not teach a dialect.** Norway has no spoken standard, and the
country is unusually relaxed about that: broadcasters, teachers and
politicians speak their own regional varieties in public. A course still has
to record *something*, so this one records Eastern (Oslo) Bokmål — the variety
closest to the written norm the course teaches, and the one you will hear most
in national broadcasting. Where a major dialect differs in a way a learner
will actually notice — the uvular *r* along the south-west coast, the "thick"
*l* inland, the retained diphthongs in the west — the pages say so and move
on.

## How to work through it

Two to three short sessions, or one lesson. Read the page, listen to each
segment twice, and read the transcript aloud with the recording the second
time. Then leave it. The point of the stage is not mastery; it is that when
Unit 1 says *Hei, jeg heter …*, you already know why *heter* has a long *e*
and *hetter* would not, and you are not silently reading a German word.
